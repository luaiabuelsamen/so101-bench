\begin{abstract}
Low-cost servo arms ship without force sensing, and standard
imitation-learning stacks observe joint positions only, so learned policies
close the gripper open-loop against contact. For logs that record
synchronized joint-position commands and measured positions --- which is
what the public SO-100/SO-101 corpus already contains --- the servo tracking
error $\delta = a[t{-}1] - s[t]$ is a contact proxy computable after the
fact, from fields those logs already carry. Current stacks discard it. In a
pre-registered six-design study at 280 demonstrations on a simulated
force-critical task (5 seeds $\times$ 100 paired episodes), every
observation carrying this channel outperforms the channel-free base, while
predicting $\delta$ as an auxiliary training target does not: the channel
must be observed, not merely learned. The strongest design appends a
free-motion-compensated residual, the \emph{lag excess}, reaching 26.6\%
success against 6.2\% ($+20.4$ points, $t = 4.7$), against a pre-declared
resolution of 11.6 points at five seeds. A position-history control with no
access to the channel reaches a comparable level, so this grid establishes
that contact-relevant temporal information helps and not that this channel
is uniquely capable. Because the lag excess reads one more frame of action
history than a one-step baseline, we also ran an
information-matched control; the advantage persists at $+11.9$ points
(three seeds; 95\% CI $[+3.3, +20.7]$), below the bar at that seed count.
On 546 episodes across 16 public teleoperation datasets the pre-registered
prediction that successful grasps carry higher $\delta$ fails: high $\delta$
at grasp time accompanies failure, consistent with operators forcing jammed
grasps, which rules out mining such corpora for success labels through this
channel alone. Code and data at [URL].
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Imitation Learning, Force Estimation, Low-Cost Manipulation}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Low-cost servo arms and open imitation-learning stacks have made
manipulation research broadly accessible: ACT on ALOHA-class
hardware~\citep{zhao2023act}, diffusion policies~\citep{chi2023diffusion},
and the LeRobot ecosystem~\citep{cadene2024lerobot} with its VLA
descendants~\citep{kim2024openvla, black2024pi0, shukor2025smolvla,
nvidia2025groot} all train behavior cloning on teleoperated
demonstrations, and all share one proprioceptive default: the current
measured joint positions, one timestep, nothing else
(Sec.~\ref{sec:related}).

This default fails at contact. The arms carry no force or tactile sensors,
so a position-only policy cannot distinguish a seated grasp from an empty
close; the failures are crushed objects, unseated grips, and drops during
transport. The information needed to distinguish these cases is present
in the logs. The servos are proportional position controllers, so when the jaw meets an object the measured position stalls
while the command deepens, and the gap reflects the servo's deflection
under load. The authors of ACT state the physics directly: force ``is
implicitly defined by the difference between'' leader and follower
positions~\citep{zhao2023act}, and a recent intervention study shows on real
hardware, across four force-critical tasks, that removing this
discrepancy signal from ACT's action labels breaks
them~\citep{wong2026beyond}. Yet the stack keeps one
operand of that subtraction in the action labels and never provides the
difference as an observation; the LeRobot Feetech driver reads only
\texttt{Present\_Position} into the state vector, and the reference ACT
implementation raises on \texttt{n\_obs\_steps != 1}~\citep{cadene2024lerobot}.

The channel has a property no current- or torque-based estimator shares:
it is recoverable retroactively. Wherever a recorded dataset's
\texttt{action} field holds joint-position commands synchronized with
\texttt{observation.state} in a common frame,
$\delta = a[t{-}1] - s[t]$ can be computed after the fact; 16 of the 20
public datasets we targeted passed our frozen inclusion gates
(Sec.~\ref{sec:corpus}, which also details the caveats on action
semantics).

That property is what separates this channel from the alternatives, and
the separation is concrete rather than rhetorical. A recent survey of
force sensing for manipulation learning organises the field into families
--- finger-mounted optical, finger force, finger audio, joint torque,
wrist force/torque, and combinations --- every one of which adds
hardware. That survey states the premise of this work in its own terms:
``current robot foundation models focus exclusively on visual input and
position control. Relying on position alone might not be sufficient to
achieve true dexterity, as small errors in position may lead to large
errors in force in stiff systems''~\citep{xie2025forceful}. It instances
Gemini Robotics, 2000--5000 episodes per task over twelve months, as not
including force. Recovering the quantity from logs never instrumented for
it is not among the options it catalogues, and the phrase ``tracking
error'' does not appear in it. Of 123 archived
SO-100/SO-101 datasets we could read, every one records
\texttt{observation.state} as joint positions alone --- no load, no
current. The remedy of \citet{wong2026beyond} needs motor current or
joint effort, which the recording stack caps at half scale on the gripper
of every follower it connects (App.~\ref{app:hardware}); FACTR~2's
estimator needs a purpose-collected dataset carrying external-force
labels~\citep{oh2026factr2}; \citet{hwang2018virtual} needs a per-servo
system identification performed on the hardware itself. None of those requirements is
satisfiable from a position-only log. The tracking error needs two fields
such logs already store.
The same signal supports runtime enforcement from bus telemetry alone
(Sec.~\ref{sec:guard}).

We ask what a small-data policy must observe to use this channel, and
compare six observation designs that differ only in which function of the
logged pair enters the state vector. The best tested form is the
\emph{lag excess} $e[t] = \delta[t] - \hat{K} r[t]$, which subtracts a
per-joint free-motion following-lag model fit on the jaw-open frames of
the training demonstrations, label-free and frozen before training
(Sec.~\ref{sec:formulation}; implementation details in
App.~\ref{app:impl}).

Three results are established at our pre-declared seed resolution
(Sec.~\ref{sec:grid}). Channel-bearing observation beats channel-free in
every input form. The tested auxiliary-prediction baseline recovers none
of the direct-input gain: the same information moves performance as an
input and not as a training target. And the lag excess is the only tested
design to beat raw action history (26.6\% vs.\ 13.8\%, CI
$[+2.8, +22.6]$), though that baseline reads one fewer frame of action
history than the lag excess does; against a control matched on those raw
inputs the margin is $+11.9$ points (CI $[+3.3, +20.7]$ at three seeds).
Whether its edge over raw $\delta$ comes from the subtraction is not
resolved at this budget, so we present it as the recommended
instantiation, not a finding about the subtraction itself. On
the public corpus, a pre-registered prediction fails: high $\delta$ at
grasp time accompanies failed grasps, and the traces attribute this to
operators over-commanding at contact (Sec.~\ref{sec:corpus}). A 40-line guard on the same telemetry removes
96\% of simulated crush events without retraining (Sec.~\ref{sec:guard}).

We summarize our contributions below:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Characterization of servo tracking error as a contact/load proxy
  with a validity envelope and a resolution-vs-margin design rule
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:channel}, \ref{sec:validity}).
  \item A pre-registered six-design observation study at 280
  demonstrations: the channel helps as an input and not as an auxiliary
  prediction target (Sec.~\ref{sec:grid}).
  \item A pre-registered 16-dataset corpus study associating high
  $\delta$ at grasp with proxy-defined failure (Sec.~\ref{sec:corpus}).
  \item A runtime jaw guard from bus telemetry alone, with paired
  deleted-success accounting (Sec.~\ref{sec:guard}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Sensorless force estimation.} Momentum-residual observers recover
external torque on torque-controlled arms~\citep{deluca2005sensorless,
haddadin2017collisions}; virtual force sensors extend the idea to
low-cost platforms~\citep{yen2019virtual}, and \citet{hwang2018virtual}
estimate torque from commanded and measured positions on hobby servos,
prior art for the raw channel --- though their estimator is built on a
dedicated per-servo dynamic system identification, so while its inference
inputs are positions, the model it needs cannot be obtained from an
archived log without access to the hardware that produced it; series-elastic actuation made
deflection-as-force a design principle~\citep{williamson1995sea}.
NeuralActuator~\citep{dou2026neuralactuator} learns per-actuator models
for external-force perception on this servo class, and
FACTR~2~\citep{oh2026factr2, liu2025factr} feeds estimated external
torque into behavior cloning on commodity arms. Our lag excess is a
deliberate special case of FACTR~2's central idea, a linear free-motion
baseline fit on positions alone; the restriction makes the signal
retroactive, since every method above consumes live current or load
telemetry, a calibration stage, or both. Their own input ablation is the
closest independent test of our premise: comparing histories of joint
positions, of positions and velocities, and of positions, velocities and
tracking error, they report that the last ``consistently outperforms the
other inputs, suggesting that tracking error provides useful information
about controller effort and actuator response.'' That is measured on
hardware, and it concerns the inputs to a force estimator rather than to
a policy --- but it is the estimator-side form of the question our
position-history control asks of a policy, namely whether joint history
already subsumes the tracking-error channel. Closest to this work,
\citet{wong2026beyond} identify the same channel and test it by
subtraction: across four real-world force-critical tasks --- surface
following, insertion, stiffness discrimination, force-based stopping ---
they remove the leader--follower discrepancy from ACT's action labels and
report severe failures in exactly the force-critical phases, restoring
competence with torque proxies drawn from motor current or joint effort.
That is the real-hardware form of the observability claim our grid can
only make in simulation, and it establishes the core of that claim
independently of us. Two differences remain, and they are the ones that
matter for a fleet of archived data. Their intervention is subtractive,
establishing that the cue matters; ours is additive, asking which
function of it a small-data policy should observe. The hardware class
differs too: they evaluate on an ALOHA-class pair with three cameras
including a wrist-mounted view, where we target arms an order of
magnitude cheaper whose archived logs carry one scene camera or none.
And their remedy estimates joint torque from motor current, $\tau = k_t
I$ --- telemetry the recording stack caps on the contact joint
(App.~\ref{app:hardware}), that none of the archived datasets in our
corpus records, and whose retroactive recovery from such logs they do
not address --- while the channel studied here needs two position fields
every one of those logs already stores.

\citet{xie2025forceful} temper this: they judge that outside a few task
classes ``the performance of imitation learning models is not at a level
of dynamics where force truly matters''. The exceptions they name are
pouring precise volumes, tight-tolerance insertion, and grasping fragile
and deformable objects --- the last being the class our force-band task
belongs to. We read that as bounding where a force channel pays rather
than as disputing that it does, and we adopt the bound: the grid measures
observation design on a force-critical task, not manipulation in
general.

\textbf{Observation design in imitation learning.}
ACT~\citep{zhao2023act}, Diffusion Policy~\citep{chi2023diffusion},
OpenVLA~\citep{kim2024openvla}, $\pi_0$~\citep{black2024pi0},
SmolVLA~\citep{shukor2025smolvla}, and GR00T~N1~\citep{nvidia2025groot}
all condition on measured joint positions at one or two timesteps; none
states a rationale for excluding actuator-error signals. The field's
argument against richer observations is causal confusion: past actions
invite copycat shortcuts~\citep{dehaan2019causal, wen2020copycat,
torne2025pasttoken}. Our grid places both effects in one table.

\textbf{Grasp monitoring and runtime safety.} Industrial grippers ship
grasp verification from actuator feedback as firmware: Robotiq's
object-detection register fires on commanded-vs-actual discrepancy under
a current limit~\citep{robotiq2019manual}, and SCHUNK advertises
workpiece-loss detection from an integrated encoder~\citep{schunk_egk};
tactile alternatives require added sensing~\citep{calandra2017feeling}.
Academic safety filters for learned policies~\citep{alshiekh2018shielding,
hsu2024safetyfilter, brunke2022safelearning} consume a dynamics model, a
state estimate, or exteroception; the guard of Sec.~\ref{sec:guard}
applies the industrial telemetry-only approach to wrap learned policies,
and we have not found a filter in the academic literature that runs from
servo-bus telemetry alone.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

Let $q[t]$ denote measured joint positions in integer encoder counts and
$g[t]$ commanded goal positions, both logged at every control step (in
LeRobot format, \texttt{observation.state} and \texttt{action}). The
tracking error and lag excess are
\begin{equation}
  \delta[t] = g[t{-}1] - q[t], \qquad
  e[t] = \delta[t] - \hat{K} \cdot r[t], \qquad r[t] = g[t{-}1] - g[t{-}2],
\end{equation}
where $r[t]$ is the one-step command rate and $\hat{K}$ is a per-joint
coefficient fit by least squares through the origin on the free-motion
(jaw-open) frames of the training demonstrations, computed once and
frozen before training (full computation, alignment convention, and
normalization in App.~\ref{app:impl}). Each servo runs a proportional
position loop, so joint torque obeys $\tau \approx K_p \cdot \delta$ and
jaw grip force obeys $F \approx \kappa \cdot \delta_{\text{jaw}}$ for a
calibration constant $\kappa$ in newtons per count. Two conditions make
$\delta$ meaningful: the logged action must be the command actually
applied to the servo, and command-to-state timing must be known and
stable. Both hold by construction in our simulated plant; on real logs
they are empirical properties of the recording stack
(Sec.~\ref{sec:corpus}). We study which function of the logged pair
$(q, g)$ a policy trained on $N = 280$ demonstrations should observe.

\subsection{The channel and its envelope}
\label{sec:channel}

On our simulated plant the jaw calibration is $\kappa = 2.00$\,N per
count, linear while the jaw is seated and quasi-static, with held-out
RMSE 4.3\,N over 4--78\,N. The same fit carries an intercept of
$-3.6$\,N. It is not a measured offset: the fitted forces span
4--78\,N, so the smallest $\delta$ the fit ever saw is near 3.8 counts and
$\delta = 0$ lies outside its domain --- the intercept is an
extrapolation artifact, which is why the proportional law of
Sec.~\ref{sec:formulation} carries none. Within the fitted range $\kappa$
converts \emph{differences} in $\delta$ to differences in force; it is used here only in the quantization rule
below, which depends on the slope alone, and a reader mapping an absolute
$\delta$ to newtons with $\kappa$ and no intercept is off by that
constant. These constants describe the modeled plant:
the simulator implements the servo loop of Sec.~\ref{sec:formulation}, so
its $\delta$-to-force map is clean by construction; real STS3215 hardware
adds stiction, backlash, deadband, saturation, and thermal drift. Bench
measurement confirms the saturation and characterizes the baseline
registers (App.~\ref{app:hardware}); the remainder is still unmeasured. What
simulation can characterize is the structure of the proxy: outside the
seated quasi-static regime the signal is not a load reading, since free
motion contributes following lag (which $e[t]$ removes) and fast
transport contributes inertial deflection
(App.~\ref{app:mech}, Fig.~\ref{fig:mech}). Encoder quantization yields a
design rule: a safe-grip window of force margin $M$ requires an observed
quantum on the order of $M / \kappa$. We state this as an order and not as
a threshold. Treated as a necessary condition it is falsified by our own
sweep, which retains most of its success at quanta coarser than
$M / \kappa$ in every margin tested; the rule fires conservatively, ahead
of the degradation it predicts, by factors of 1.2--3.1 across three
margins (Sec.~\ref{sec:validity}). What survives is the mechanism: the
failure is a cliff, and its location moves with the margin.

\subsection{System, task, and data}
\label{sec:system}

All experiments except Sec.~\ref{sec:corpus} run on a simulated
STS3215-class arm (five joints plus a one-DoF jaw, six servos) with a
calibrated contact model. The task is pick-and-place of an object whose
retention requires grip force above a lift threshold and whose integrity
fails above a crush threshold. A privileged-force oracle passes an
admission test on this task and, as a negative control, shows that
standard rigid-block pick-and-place cannot distinguish force-aware from
force-blind control at all. Demonstrations come from a scripted
closed-loop teacher (280 episodes, success-filtered) whose grip regulator
acts on the same bus-observable tracking error the arms study, with
per-episode grip-depth targets drawn from $U[12, 45]$ counts and injected
recovery perturbations; no privileged force sensing enters collection, the
teacher's commands are the applied commands by construction, and the
observation arms see only functions of $(q, g)$. Policies are
ACT~\citep{zhao2023act}, trained for 12{,}000 steps at 96\,px, identical
across arms; at
this resolution we cannot rule out a camera deformation cue, but the base
arm's floor performance shows any such cue is insufficient alone. Seed
variance was measured before any comparison:
$\sigma_{\text{seed}} \approx 10$ points at $n = 100$ evaluation
episodes, so 5-seed comparisons resolve gaps of roughly 11 points and we
claim nothing finer. Evaluation episodes are common-random-number paired:
every arm and seed rolls out the identical episode sequence.

\subsection{Observation designs}
\label{sec:arms}

Six arms share the training recipe and differ only in the proprioceptive
observation. \textbf{Base (A).} Measured positions $s[t]$ only: the stock
LeRobot observation. \textbf{History (B).} $s[t]$ plus the previous
action $a[t{-}1]$: the minimal change that makes $\delta$ recoverable in
principle, and the arm the copycat literature~\citep{dehaan2019causal,
wen2020copycat} warns about. \textbf{Delta (C).} $s[t]$ plus raw
$\delta[t]$: the channel with its motion confound intact.
\textbf{Auxiliary target (D).} $s[t]$ only, with $\delta[t]$ predicted as
an auxiliary target through a small head at loss weight 0.1: the same
information as supervision rather than input. \textbf{Lag excess (E).}
$s[t]$ plus $e[t]$: the channel with free-motion lag subtracted.
\textbf{Seat token (F).} $s[t]$ plus a latched binary seat bit from the
guard's frozen stall detector run causally over $(s[t], a[t{-}1])$: the
channel compressed to one bit.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate: \emph{(i) over what envelope is $\delta$ a valid
contact/load proxy; (ii) which observation design exploits it at small
data; (iii) does the channel predict grasp outcomes on the public
corpus?}

\subsection{Channel behavior on the simulated plant}
\label{sec:validity}

The calibration of Sec.~\ref{sec:channel} holds in the seated
quasi-static regime. Because the simulator implements the servo loop that
generates $\delta$, these numbers validate the proxy against our own
plant model; their value is in mapping where the proxy's structure
breaks. A stall detector on net travel and lag excess localizes seat
events, and constructed adversarial negatives (free-space reversals,
transport shakes) do not fire it within the declared envelope. Coarsening
the observed channel eventually collapses force-aware grasping, and
rerunning the sweep at a different margin moves the cliff accordingly ---
which is the signature separating a resolution limit from the mere
deletion of input bits. The collapse does not occur at $M / \kappa$: at a
100\,N limit the channel holds 92\% and 75\% of its baseline placements at
quanta of 4 and 8, both coarser than the predicted threshold, and the
observed cliff runs 1.2--3.1$\times$ beyond the prediction across the
three margins. The scale constant here is the pre-registered
2.4\,N/count of the sweep rather than the $\kappa = 2.00$ of
Sec.~\ref{sec:channel}; the discrepancy is reported rather than
reconciled, since refitting a constant frozen before the sweep ran would
defeat the pre-registration. \texttt{TYPICAL\_PEAK\_N}, a single median
standing in for a contact peak-grip distribution spanning 19--56\,N, is
the likely source, and the rule should be re-derived from that
distribution before it is claimed quantitatively.

Three results resolve. First, channel-bearing observation beats
channel-free in every input form: C, E, and F each clear the base by at
least 15 points with intervals excluding zero (Table~\ref{tab:grid}).
Second, the tested auxiliary-prediction design does not recover the
direct-input gain: arm D lands equal to base while the same information
as an input triples success; other auxiliary weightings and architectures
could in principle close this gap, and none was tuned here. Third, in the
comparison declared as headline before the confound below was known, only
the lag-excess form beats action history at resolution, by 12.8 points
(Welch's $t = 2.6$ across seed means, against a bar of 11.6 at five
seeds); raw $\delta$'s 8-point margin over history does not resolve. That
pair is confounded: $E$ consumes $a[t{-}2]$ through the commanded rate and
the baseline does not, so it varies content as well as form, and it is
superseded by the information-matched control $B2 = [s[t], a[t{-}1],
a[t{-}2]]$, which $E$ exceeds by $+11.9$ (three seeds, CI $[+3.3,
+20.7]$, below the three-seed bar of 15). We report $E{-}B$ because it was
declared, and rest the form claim on $E{-}B2$. The
remaining pairs stay open, including E against C (difference $+4.8$, CI
$[-6.2, +15.8]$), so whether E's edge comes from the subtraction or from
$\delta$ at all is not attributable at this budget;
resolving $E{-}C$ by seed expansion would require roughly 40--60 seeds
per arm at this $\sigma_{\text{seed}}$, and evaluation is already
episode-paired. The frozen prediction named E the sharpest arm and
expected D, E, and F to clear B; D's share of that prediction failed, as
reported above. An interim 3-seed analysis flagged E's margin as resolved
and F's as at-the-floor, and the expansion to 5 seeds confirmed both
calls, collapsing F's margin over B and preserving E's. Caveats: five seed means per arm, a
headline difference of similar magnitude to the declared resolution, and
uncorrected secondary comparisons beyond the designated primary one.
A best-seed robustness probe under object-property shifts (block mass
$\times 0.5$ and $\times 1.5$, friction $\times 0.5$; $n{=}50$ paired
episodes per condition, orderings only) shows the channel arms' margins
persist under every shift while the channel-free base returns
byte-identical outcomes in all conditions -- the signature of a policy
that never interacts with the object's dynamics -- supporting the reading
that channel-bearing policies regulate contact rather than replay a
visual sequence (protocol and per-condition table in the repository).

Within those limits the ordering supports one design statement: at small
data on this force-critical task, observing the channel is the change
that moves performance, and the free-motion-compensated form is the best
tested way to observe it.

\subsection{Ecosystem study: a pre-registered negative}
\label{sec:corpus}

Does $\delta$ predict grasp outcomes corpus-wide? The protocol was frozen
before the run: seat detection by the stall detector of
Sec.~\ref{sec:validity}, $\delta$-at-seat as unsigned tracking error,
success proxy defined as the gripper staying closed through transport,
and the prediction that successes carry higher $\delta$ with Cohen's
$d > 0.5$ in at least 10 of 20 datasets. Of the 20 targeted
SO-100/SO-101 datasets, 16 passed the frozen inclusion gates with
connectivity: 546 episodes, 283 proxy successes.

\textbf{The prediction fails: 2/16 datasets} (pooled within-dataset
$d = -0.39$). The channel is informative with the opposite sign:
$|d| > 0.5$ in 10/16 datasets, 7/16 significantly inverted
(Fig.~\ref{fig:corpus}). The traces explain the physics. Teleop leader
devices have no hard stop, so operators over-command every close; the
seat flag fires in nearly every episode of 14/16 datasets because empty
jaws stall at the mechanical limit exactly as loaded ones do. Successful
grasps are stereotyped low-$\delta$ closes (in one dataset every clean
episode rests at the same 7-count gap), while failures carry
40--80-count frames of an operator forcing a jammed grasp. High $\delta$
at grasp time is associated with failure under the frozen proxy. This
rules out mining the corpus for success labels through this channel
alone, and it supports enforcement: the high-$\delta$ events the corpus
associates with failure are what the guard caps.

The result also bounds Sec.~\ref{sec:grid}'s transfer to retroactive
training on real teleop data. When the seat event fires in essentially
every episode, a latched seat bit (arm F) carries no information, and raw
$\delta$ at close time reads commanded depth as much as contact; what
stays informative is $\delta$ magnitude and its dynamics, which is what
the inverted effect sizes measure. Whether the lag-excess form retains
its simulated advantage when trained on over-commanded human
demonstrations is an open empirical question, not a corollary. Two
caveats attach to the corpus numbers: the recorded \texttt{action} is the
leader position, which approximates but need not equal the applied
follower command, and action-to-state timing is characterized only up to
an integer-offset sweep over $k \in \{0, \dots, 5\}$, which leaves these
conclusions unchanged (the median per-dataset shift in Cohen's $d$ across
offsets is 0.12) while estimating the physical alignment at three to four
recorded frames (App.~\ref{app:impl}).

\subsection{Runtime guard}
\label{sec:guard}

The same telemetry supports enforcement. A 40-line jaw guard (net-travel
stall and lag-excess seat detection, a 12-count squeeze budget past seat,
a closing-rate limit) runs from bus signals with no dynamics model, no
retraining, and no object knowledge; all results are simulated. Paired
its detection thresholds make it insensitive to closes slower than its
rate gates, so it engages on aggressive closers and not on the gentle
scripted teacher (App.~\ref{app:guard}), and on three learned checkpoints
over identical paired episodes its
first-generation detector (App.~\ref{app:guard}) reduced crush events
from 85 to 3 of 180 (96\%) across the crush-capable tiers, with median
peak force over those same episodes dropping from 33--92\,N to
13--26\,N. It also removes every success in the experiment: 12 of 270
unguarded become 0 of 270, and no guarded cell scores above zero. The
policies are weak to begin with here (4.4\% unguarded, and the
channel-free arm scores zero either way), so this is the guard deleting
the few successes that existed rather than dismantling a working policy
--- but a wrapper that never succeeds is not yet a deployable one. The
shape of the trade is not peculiar to us: \citet{nakamura2025latentcbf}
report a least-restrictive latent safety filter on hardware that drives
failures from 62\% to zero while stalls rise from zero to 62\% and task
success is unchanged at 38\%, converting every failure into a stall and
adding nothing. Ours is the more severe instance of that mode, since it
removes the successes as well, and the comparison names what a deployable
version needs rather than excusing what ours does: intervention selective
enough to leave the succeeding trajectories intact. Demonstrations must record applied
commands, and wrapped controllers must be closed-loop on contact.
Details, figure, and integration accounting in App.~\ref{app:guard}. On
hardware the register this guard would otherwise read is capped by the
recording stack itself and saturates through contact, while the channel
does not (App.~\ref{app:hardware}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

All learning and guard results are simulation, on one gripper and one
object family; every quantity in newtons inherits the simulator's servo
and contact model until bench calibration lands
(App.~\ref{app:hardware}). The task is constructed to require force-band
control, so the grid measures observation design on a force-critical
task, not manipulation in general. The lag-excess model is a first-order
correction; an offline ladder of richer free-motion predictors (intercept,
direction and acceleration terms, short causal FIR) improves held-out
free-motion residual by at most 1.9\% over the scalar model on the jaw,
so the linear choice is not leaving obvious compensation on the table,
though learned predictors in the style of~\citet{oh2026factr2} remain
untested as policy inputs. The seed budget leaves $C{-}B$ and
$E{-}C$ open, so the value of the subtraction itself is not established.

The most consequential open question concerns operating point. The grid
runs at 280 demonstrations, 96\,px and 12k steps. At a larger budget --- 600
demonstrations, 224\,px, 50k steps, where absolute success reaches 57--63\%
rather than the 6--27\% reported here --- a two-seed comparison of the raw
channel against the action-history baseline is indistinguishable (60\% vs.\
57\%, with a within-policy seed spread of 12 points, four times the
between-policy gap). That cell tested $C$ against $B$; $E$, the design this
paper recommends, has not been run at that budget. Two readings are
therefore live and we cannot currently separate them: that the channel's
advantage is a small-data effect which disappears where policies become
useful, or that the free-motion subtraction is precisely what survives
scaling and the raw form is not. What the small-data ladder does support
independently of either is that policies with no action-history input in
any form plateau at 16--24\% lift commitment across three data recipes
while channel-bearing policies reach 47--76\%: the information appears
necessary, and it is the \emph{form} whose value at scale is unresolved.
This pattern is not peculiar to our setup:
\citet{zeng2026revisiting} sweep context length against dataset size and find
the ordering between short- and long-context policies reverses between 200 and
1000 demonstrations, with dataset size disproportionately favouring the
longer-context policy. Their own recommendation applies directly to us: they observe that
datasets of 50--100 demonstrations remain common and ``encourage
evaluating results across dataset sizes to ensure results hold at
scale''. Our 280-to-600 window sits inside their crossover, so the tie we
observe is the expected behaviour of a known effect rather than an
unexplained inconsistency --- which also means a single budget cannot identify
the mechanism, and any arm we compare must be compared at more than one.

A second caveat bears on the comparison declared as primary. The lag excess
$e[t] = \delta[t] - \hat{K} r[t]$ depends on $a[t{-}2]$ through the commanded
rate, while the action-history baseline $B$ observes $s[t]$ and $a[t{-}1]$
only, and $\hat{K}$ is far from zero (0.74--0.84 across joints, 0.80 on the
jaw). $E$ therefore consumes one more frame of action history than the
baseline it is compared against, and $E{-}B$ confounds the free-motion
subtraction with that additional frame. The comparison that isolates the
subtraction holds the raw inputs fixed: $E$ against a two-step raw history
$B2 = [s[t], a[t{-}1], a[t{-}2]]$, information-matched to $E$ by construction.
We ran it at three seeds under a rule frozen beforehand. $B2$ reaches
$14.7 \pm 3.2$ and $E$ exceeds it by $+11.9$ ($t = 3.03$, CI
$[+3.3, +20.7]$): against a control with identical raw inputs the advantage
persists, which answers the confound directly. We decline the available
decomposition. $B2$ over the one-frame baseline is $+0.9$ with CI
$[-7.7, +9.0]$, a null wide enough to be consistent with the extra frame
carrying most of $E{-}B$ or none of it, so the point estimates do not
partition $E{-}B$ and we do not report them as though they did. Neither
difference clears the 15-point bar declared in advance, and $B2$ is
18-dimensional against $E$'s 12, so the comparison is matched in information
and not in width.

A second control bears on the mechanism and does not favour the strong
reading. Arm $G$ observes $[s[t], s[t{-}8]]$ --- joint history only, from
which $\delta$ is not computable even in principle --- and is width-matched
to $E$. At three seeds it reaches $20.3 \pm 4.7$. It is indistinguishable
from the raw-channel arm $C$ ($-1.5$, $t = -0.29$) and from $E$ itself
($E{-}G = +6.3$, CI $[-3.2, +16.0]$), while clearing the channel-free base
by $+14.1$ ($t = 3.73$, CI $[+5.9, +22.1]$) --- 69\% of $E$'s own margin
over that base. The ordering across arms is therefore base 6.2, position
history 20.3, raw channel 21.8, lag excess 26.6: the large step is from no
temporal information to some, and the step from generic history to this
channel does not resolve at this budget. Under the declared bar,
$E{-}G$ would need roughly 18 seeds.

We state the consequence plainly. What this grid establishes is that an
observation beyond $s[t]$ is necessary; \emph{which} form it must take is
open, and the reading on which the servo tracking error specifically is the
enabling observation is not established by these experiments. That
proposition and the long-context account of \citet{zeng2026revisiting} are
both consistent with what we measured. We do not claim that position
history fails to substitute for the channel; at three seeds we cannot
distinguish the two, which is a different statement and the one the data
supports.
$C{-}B$ is by contrast a clean representation test, since $C$ and $B$ observe
the same raw quantities --- and it does not resolve at our seed budget
($+8.0$, $t = 1.44$). The decomposition is exact:
$E{-}B = (E{-}C) + (C{-}B) = +4.8\ (t{=}0.86) + 8.0\ (t{=}1.44) = +12.8$, so
the one pair clearing our resolution bar is significant only as the sum of two
components neither of which is separately claimable at $n=5$ seeds. A wider
audit of the arms' information dependence sharpens this: of the design
comparisons the grid draws conclusions from, only $C{-}B$ and $D{-}A$ hold raw
content fixed while varying form. $F$ in particular consumes an entire episode
prefix, because its seat detector latches once and never resets, yet does not
beat $E$ --- so a simple information ordering does not explain the grid either.
$D{-}A$ is matched by construction, the channel entering only as an auxiliary
target, which makes the observe-versus-predict null the grid's most securely
grounded design result. Nothing here bears on the channel-versus-channel-free
result ($E{-}A = +20.4$, $t = 4.7$); what is unresolved is the narrower claim
that the lag excess specifically improves on action history. Running $E$ and
the matched two-step baseline at both budgets is the experiment we would
prioritize.
On real logs, $\delta$ depends on action semantics and timing that the
corpus study characterizes only up to an integer-offset sweep. The corpus
success proxy is unvalidated against ground truth, and multi-grasp tasks
were excluded by design. The grid is measured on one demonstration
collection; a three-seed recollection (App.~\ref{app:guard}) surfaces
collection-level resampling variance that our comparisons do not yet
average over. The channel is valid only seated and quasi-static.

%===============================================================================

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Position-command logs contain a retrospectively computable contact proxy:
the servo tracking error, valid within a quasi-static envelope our
simulated plant makes measurable. At 280 demonstrations, policies must
observe this channel; predicting it as the tested auxiliary target
recovers none of the gain, and the free-motion-compensated form is the
best tested instantiation. Across 16 public datasets high $\delta$ at
grasp time accompanies failure, which rules out free outcome labels from
this channel alone and supports its use for enforcement. What remains
open requires hardware: bench calibration, retroactive training on real
teleop logs, and a copycat-style probe of when history helps versus hurts
in behavior cloning.

\section{Channel computation and implementation details}
\label{app:impl}

All signals are in integer encoder counts at the recorded cadence
(stride-2 frames of a 30\,Hz bus, i.e.\ 15\,Hz):
$\delta_j[t] = a_j[t{-}1] - s_j[t]$, where $a$ is the commanded and $s$
the measured position, the $k{=}1$ alignment convention. An offset sweep
over $k \in \{0, \dots, 5\}$ on the public corpus leaves every
Sec.~\ref{sec:corpus} conclusion unchanged (the median per-dataset shift
in Cohen's $d$ across offsets is 0.12) while estimating the physical
alignment at three to four recorded frames; the bench latency measurement
(App.~\ref{app:hardware}) will pin it. The commanded rate is the first difference of commands,
$r_j[t] = a_j[t{-}1] - a_j[t{-}2]$, zero at the first two frames of an
episode. The lag model is a single per-joint scalar through the origin,
$\hat{K}_j = \sum r\delta / \sum r^2$, fit over frames whose jaw command
exceeds its 30th percentile; the jaw is the task's only contact, so
jaw-open frames are free motion. Fitted values span
$\hat{K} = 0.74$--$0.84$ across joints on our training set (and move by at
most $0.09$ when the free-motion percentile criterion is varied from the
10th to the 50th), computed once on the training demonstrations and
frozen. The policy input
is the concatenation
$[s_{\text{norm}},\, (\delta - \hat{K} r - \mu_\delta)/\sigma_\delta]$;
joint states and actions are standardized by dataset statistics, while
$\delta$, $e$, and the aux target use a fixed scale of
$\sigma_\delta = 8$ counts with $\mu_\delta = 0$, so no channel statistic
leaks from evaluation. The same statistics are saved beside the
checkpoint and applied identically at evaluation, where $a[t{-}1]$ is the
previously issued command, so the feature is computable from exactly what
the deployed stack already has. Appended channels concatenate onto the
state vector, leaving the network otherwise unchanged.

\section{Guard details}
\label{app:guard}

The guard combines net-travel stall and lag-excess seat detection, a
squeeze budget of 12 counts ($\approx +24$\,N on the simulated plant)
past seat, and a closing-rate limit. Two regimes follow from the
detector's thresholds. On aggressive closers it binds: learned policies
commanding fast or deep closes are capped near 40\,N against raw peaks of
99--127\,N, and an open-loop clamp reference commanding 360\,N grips is
held to 10--28\,N. On the gentle closed-loop teacher ($\sim$0.3 counts
per frame in its fine pass, below both detection gates) it never fires: a
paired run shows 25/30 placements wrapped versus 28/30 raw at
statistically indistinguishable $\sim$39\,N grips, i.e.\ no enforcement
occurred and the run measures wiring overhead only. Whether
threshold-level insensitivity to gentle closes is acceptable
conservatism or a coverage gap depends on the deployment's hazard model;
we state it as a measured property of these thresholds.

An independently recollected demonstration set (same teacher, recipe, and
budget; fresh episodes) trained at three seeds per arm neither resolves
any per-arm difference from the original grid nor reproduces its
point-estimate ordering at that reduced seed count (the lag-excess arm's
fresh-collection seeds span 7--27 against the original 17--36, Welch's
$t = 1.6$). Across the six arms the three channel-bearing designs' point
estimates fell while the three channel-free designs' rose, none
individually resolving; four marginal statistics of the two collections
(tracking-error distributions, command floors, command-rate
distributions, per-episode grip-depth diversity) are indistinguishable,
so we treat this as unattributed collection-level resampling variance --
a component above seed variance that the headline comparison of
Sec.~\ref{sec:grid} does not yet average over, and that hardening it
requires spanning with seed expansion over multiple collections.
The crush-elimination A/B predates the detector's final revision: on
three learned checkpoints over identical paired episodes the
first-generation detector (single-step stall test, 4-count budget)
reduced crush events from 85 to 3 of 180 across the two crush-capable
tiers (Fig.~\ref{fig:guard}), with median peak force over those same 180
episodes dropping from 33--92\,N to 13--26\,N; across all nine cells,
including the no-crush tier where crushing is impossible, the unguarded
range is 33--131\,N. A rerun under the final detector is pending
alongside hardware validation. The guard also deletes successes, and it
deletes all of them: unguarded totals are 12 successes in 270 episodes,
guarded totals are 0 in 270, and every one of the nine cells scores
0/30 guarded. The losses fall on two arms --- the quantized arm gives up
7/30 at a 131\,N median and 1/30 at the 120\,N tier, the delta arm 2/30
and 2/30 --- while the channel-free arm scores zero with and without the
guard. Since the unguarded rate is 4.4\%, this is a guard removing the
few successes that existed rather than a working policy being
dismantled, but it means the enforcement layer as tested trades all
measured task success for a 96\% crush reduction. Integration requires two
properties: demonstrations must record applied commands rather than
intentions, and any wrapped controller must be closed-loop on contact,
since the rate limiter breaks open-loop grasp timing by construction.
Collecting demonstrations under \emph{active} guard enforcement would
require a detector variant sensitive to gentle closes and remains
untested.

\section{Hardware: the reviewer's baseline on a physical STS3215}
\label{app:hardware}

The first question this paper invites is why estimate contact from
tracking error when the servo already reports \texttt{Present\_Load} and
\texttt{Present\_Current} over the same bus. This appendix answers that
question on hardware and states plainly what it does not answer: the
newton scale is not yet measured, so every force quantity in the body
still inherits the simulator's contact model.

\subsection{Static: the register is the channel, rescaled}

\textbf{Protocol.} A payload staircase on one STS3215 in an SO-101
shoulder-lift joint. Twelve hanging masses spanning 63.7--319\,g, each held
at five arm poses, logging \texttt{Present\_Load} (address 60),
\texttt{Present\_Current} (69), \texttt{Present\_Position} (56) and
\texttt{Goal\_Position} (42) at static equilibrium;
$\delta = \texttt{Goal} - \texttt{Present}$, in encoder counts. Masses are
known from package labelling rather than weighed, so their systematic error
is unquantified. Three masses were measured twice
(106, 149, 191\,g), and those replicates are used below as the
repeatability estimate; they bound the random component only.

\textbf{Poses are fit separately.} Pooling them regresses across five
different lever arms at once and inflates every residual. All intervals
below are 95\% bootstrap percentiles over 10{,}000 resamples of the twelve
masses.

\textbf{The register carries the same information as the channel.}
Pooled over all sixty measurements,
$\texttt{Present\_Load} = -4.70\,(\pm 0.10)\,\delta + 0.1$ with
$R^2 = 0.976$ $[0.969, 0.982]$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:appd}, right). Per pose the
two track the true mass to within 0.017 of each other in $R^2$, agreeing to
three decimals in three of five poses. They are one signal on this joint, so
the register cannot be a better instrument than the channel --- only a
differently scaled one. This is the claim the appendix is for, and it is
the one the data supports most strongly.

\textbf{What limits it is repeatability, not lever arm.} The spread in
Table~\ref{tab:appd} is large, and it does not follow slope magnitude: pose
2 has the steepest response to mass and still resolves poorly, while pose 3
has a middling slope and the tightest fit. It follows the replicate column
instead. Dividing repeatability by slope gives the mass a single
measurement can distinguish: about 17\,g at pose 3 and 41\,g at pose 1,
against 160--230\,g at the three poorly repeating poses. The channel's
usable resolution on this joint is therefore a property of where the arm is
standing, not a single number, and the favourable figure is the exception
rather than the summary.

\textbf{Current is not established as the better instrument, or the worse
one.} $\delta$ attains the higher $R^2$ in four of five poses, which under an
exact paired sign test is $p = 0.375$ --- at $n = 5$ poses that is
uninformative in either direction, four coin flips landing the same way. We
report the ordering and claim nothing from it.

\subsection{Dynamic: the baseline loses its range exactly at contact}

Fifty teleoperated demonstrations on the physical arm pair (21{,}476 frames,
a pick-and-place with a single overhead camera and no wrist view) show what
the static staircase cannot. \texttt{Present\_Load} pins at 500 in 16.5\% of
frames, and all fifty episodes reach it. The saturation is not spread evenly:
it coincides with contact, where $|\delta|$ averages $12.35^\circ$ against
$2.09^\circ$ elsewhere. Inside those frames the register reports a single
constant value while $\delta$ still spans $8.01$--$31.01^\circ$, twenty-three
degrees of range the baseline cannot express. Where the register is not
clipped the two agree as the static data predicts ($r = -0.853$
unsaturated, $-0.922$ overall).

\subsection{The ceiling belongs to the stack, not to this arm}

Reading the torque-limit register (address 48) returns 1000 for joints one
through five and 500 for the gripper alone. \texttt{Present\_Load} is
full-scale 1000, so 500 is a configured cap on the one joint that makes
contact. It is not a local misconfiguration: LeRobot~\citep{cadene2024lerobot}
writes it for every SO-100/SO-101 follower it connects, inside the
configuration path that connection always invokes, together with a matching
halving of the current-protection register --- both justified, reasonably, as
burnout protection.

Both baselines are therefore capped, on the contact joint, by the library the
fleet records through. $\delta$ requires no register and no limit; it is
arithmetic over two numbers every one of those logs already stores, and is
untouched by the same configuration.

The register read is from one arm, and we state the limit rather than leave
it inferred: a single follower reading 500 on its gripper is equally
consistent with that unit having been flashed that way. The load-bearing
evidence is therefore the code path, which writes the value unconditionally
on every connection, not the measurement, which confirms the value is present
on the one unit we have. We ran the discriminating test and it did not
discriminate. The leader configuration path performs no torque-limit write, so
a leader gripper at full scale beside a follower gripper at 500 would have
shown the stack writing the cap; instead both grippers on our pair read 500,
with joints one through five reading 1000 on each. Two explanations survive
and our hardware cannot separate them: the units may ship with the gripper
capped, or the leader may at some point have been connected through a follower
class --- a port swap this bench's own tooling explicitly anticipates --- which
would have written the value and erased the evidence permanently.

The claim therefore rests on the source rather than on our measurement.
LeRobot writes 500 on every follower it connects, which any reader can check
in the configuration path, and our registers are consistent with that without
confirming it. A clean test needs a follower that has never been connected
through the library, and we did not have one.

\subsection{What is still not measured}

Four gaps, stated because they bound the claims above.

No load cell has been applied, so the newton scale is unmeasured and the
simulator's 2.0\,N/count remains the provenance of every force figure in
the body. Hanging masses give torque in the gravity direction on one joint;
they do not give newtons per count in the jaw's closing direction, which is
the quantity the task actually depends on. The static staircase is one
joint, leaving stiffness measured at $n=1$ and its per-servo-versus-per-joint
prediction untested. It is static only: no three-speed sweep, no opening
and closing branches for hysteresis, no drift checks, and no
command-to-state latency distribution --- the last of which
Sec.~\ref{sec:formulation}'s alignment argument still resolves by corpus
sweep rather than by measurement. Finally, the current half of the
saturation result is inferred from the configuration write rather than
observed, since these recordings log position and load but not current.

Comparison rows for a completed calibration remain the simulator's
2.0\,N/count, \citet{hwang2018virtual}, and
NeuralActuator~\citep{dou2026neuralactuator}.
